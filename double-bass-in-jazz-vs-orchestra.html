Jazz bass and orchestral bass: the real differences

Same instrument, two trades. Action height, strings, amplification and what the bass is for all differ between the jazz rhythm section and the orchestra.

2026-07-17

Short answer

The instrument is the same but almost everything around it differs. Orchestral players bow, use high-tension strings and higher action, and read fixed parts. Jazz players pluck, set the action low, amplify, and improvise a bass line. Setup choices for one make the other harder.

What the bass is for

In an orchestra the bass section is a single voice made of several people. Eight players produce one line, and the work is about matching: same bow speed, same contact point, same length of note, same moment of release. Individual sound is subordinated to the section. The part is written down, the conductor decides the tempo, and the bass's job is the harmonic floor and the rhythmic weight underneath everything else.

In a jazz group the bass is one person and usually the only one doing that job. The line is not written; it is built in real time from the chord symbols, and it simultaneously states the harmony, marks the beat, and creates forward motion. A walking line has to be a coherent melody and a metronome at once. The other players listen to it for both functions, so a bassist who wanders has broken two things, not one.

The difference in responsibility shows up in how each discipline practises. Orchestral practice is heavy on excerpts, bowing, intonation in ensemble, and precision. Jazz practice is heavy on harmony, time, transcription and the physical endurance of playing pizzicato for three hours.

Setup: the practical fork in the road

String height is the clearest split. An orchestral bass is set relatively high, because bow weight pressed into a low-set string makes it buzz against the fingerboard. A jazz bass is set low, because a plucking hand pulling a high-set string for a whole night is exhausting and slows everything down. A luthier setting up a bass will ask which you do, and the answer changes the bridge.

Strings follow the same logic. Orchestral players generally use high-tension steel strings that speak cleanly under the bow and project. Jazz players often prefer lower-tension steel, or gut and gut-imitation strings, which give a rounder, shorter, thumpier pizzicato sound with less sustain and much less bow clarity. Gut is also more comfortable under the fingers at speed.

This is why doubling is genuinely inconvenient. A bass set up well for orchestral bowing is hard work to walk on, and a bass set up for jazz buzzes when you lean on it with a bow. Players who need both usually compromise in the middle, keep two instruments, or accept that one side of their playing is fighting the equipment.

Bow versus fingers

Orchestral technique is fundamentally a bow discipline. Everything about sound production, articulation, dynamics and blend runs through the right arm, and section playing demands that everyone's bow does the same thing at the same time. Pizzicato appears, but it is an occasional colour.

Jazz technique is fundamentally a right-hand pizzicato discipline: one or two fingers pulling across the string, with the sound shaped by where you pluck, how much flesh you use and how quickly you release. Getting a big, round, consistent note out of a plucked string for hours without injury is its own craft. The bow does appear in jazz, especially in ballads and in the solo playing of players like Paul Chambers, but it is the exception.

Left-hand technique is broadly shared, with one difference of emphasis. Orchestral repertoire lives lower on the instrument more often; jazz soloing spends a lot of time in thumb position on the upper strings. Both require the same fingering system underneath.

Amplification and the room

An orchestral bass is never amplified. The section is loud because there are several of them and because the hall is built to carry the sound. Everything the player does about projection happens acoustically through bow contact and instrument choice.

A jazz bass is almost always amplified, because a drum kit and a saxophone will bury an acoustic bass in any club. That introduces a whole second set of equipment decisions: a piezo pickup under the bridge wing or a magnetic pickup, a preamp, and an amplifier that reproduces low frequencies without turning them to mud. It also introduces feedback, which is the reason many jazz players prefer laminated instruments. A carved top resonates more freely and therefore howls more readily at stage volume.

The practical consequence is that a jazz bassist is also a sound engineer, and has to know how their rig behaves in a small room with hard walls versus a large one with carpet.

Reading, and whether the two can be combined

Orchestral work requires fluent reading of a written part, including sightreading at rehearsal, understanding bowing markings, and following a conductor. Jazz work requires reading chord symbols, understanding harmonic function well enough to build a line, hearing form, and often learning material by ear from recordings. The two literacies overlap less than people assume.

They are combinable. A good number of professionals do both, and the skills reinforce each other: orchestral training gives jazz players intonation and bow control that most self-taught players never develop, and jazz training gives orchestral players a sense of time and harmony that makes them better ensemble musicians.

What is not combinable is doing both badly. Each side is a full discipline, and a player who splits attention evenly for the first few years tends to end up passable at both and convincing at neither. Most teachers, ours in Singapore included, suggest choosing a centre of gravity first and adding the other once the basic technique is stable.

Frequently asked

Can I use the same double bass for jazz and orchestra?

Yes, but the setup is a compromise. Orchestral playing wants higher string height and high-tension bowing strings; jazz wants low action and softer pizzicato strings. A middle setup works for both without being ideal for either. Players who do a lot of both often end up with two instruments.

Do jazz bassists need to bow?

Not strictly, but it helps enormously. Bowing exposes intonation problems that plucking hides, so arco practice makes a jazz player's pitch more reliable. It also opens up ballad playing and solo work. Many respected jazz bassists bow well and use it sparingly in performance.

Why do jazz bassists use plywood basses?

Durability and feedback resistance, mainly. Laminated instruments tolerate transport, temperature swings and humid clubs far better than carved ones, and their more focused, less resonant top is less likely to feed back when amplified at stage volume next to a drum kit. The tonal trade-off matters less once you are through a pickup.
