Where tango actually came from

Tango was born in the port districts of Buenos Aires and Montevideo in the 1880s, out of habanera, candombe and milonga. Here is how it really happened.

2026-08-04

Short answer

Tango formed in the port neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires and Montevideo in the 1880s and 1890s, among immigrants and the descendants of enslaved Africans. It grew out of the Cuban habanera, Afro-Uruguayan candombe, the rural milonga and Andalusian song, and settled into its familiar form between 1900 and 1920.

A port, and everybody in it

Tango did not come from one place or one people. It came from a traffic jam. Between roughly 1870 and 1914 Argentina took in something close to six million immigrants, most of them Italian and Spanish, with Germans, Poles, Jews from the Russian Empire, Syrians and Lebanese arriving alongside them. Buenos Aires roughly quintupled in size. Across the river, Montevideo did a smaller version of the same thing. Both cities put the new arrivals into conventillos — old houses subdivided into single rooms around a shared courtyard, one family per room, one tap for everybody.

That courtyard matters more than any story about brothels. It is where a Neapolitan who played mandolin lived ten feet from a Black Uruguayan who played candombe drums and a criollo from the countryside who sang milonga with a guitar. Nobody was fusing anything on purpose. They were just close enough to hear each other every night, and young enough to want to dance.

The ingredients, named honestly

The clearest rhythmic ancestor is the habanera, which came up from Cuba through sheet music and sailors in the mid-1800s and had already spread across Europe — Bizet put one in Carmen in 1875. Its limping dotted figure is the seed of almost everything tango does with a bass line.

The second ancestor is candombe, the drumming and dance of Afro-Uruguayan and Afro-Argentine communities in the Rio de la Plata. Its influence is usually the least credited and the most audible: the forward-leaning accent, the weight on the low end, the sense that the beat is being pushed rather than counted. The word tango itself was in use for Black dance gatherings in the region before it named this music.

Add the milonga campera — the fast, talkative song of the pampas, guitar-driven, with a text that runs on. Add Andalusian tango and the Spanish zarzuela tunes playing in the theatres. Tango is what happened when all of that was played by ear, at speed, for money.

The Old Guard, 1895 to 1920

The first tango groups were small and improvised: violin, flute and guitar, sometimes a harp, playing from memory because most of the players could not read. This period is the Guardia Vieja, the Old Guard. It produced the first tangos anybody still knows. Angel Villoldo's El Choclo dates from around 1903; Rosendo Mendizabal's El Entrerriano from 1897. In Montevideo, a young Gerardo Matos Rodriguez wrote La Cumparsita in 1916 — the most recorded tango ever written, by a student who was not really a tango musician.

The decisive arrival was the bandoneon, a German instrument that reached the river ports with immigrants in the last decades of the century. It pushed the flute out. The flute had made early tango sound cheeky and fast; the bandoneon made it sound like something had gone wrong in your life, and the music has never fully recovered. By about 1911 Vicente Greco was already calling his group an orquesta tipica criolla, and the line-up that would define the next fifty years — bandoneons, violins, piano, double bass — was taking shape.

Paris, and the laundering of a reputation

Respectable Buenos Aires wanted nothing to do with tango. It was music from the edges, played in cheap dance halls, academias and yes, brothels, and the upper class treated it accordingly. What changed that was not a local conversion. It was Paris.

Around 1911 to 1913 tango became a craze in French society — tango teas, tango dresses, tango lessons for people with titles. The panic that followed was almost as useful as the fashion; when clergy and newspapers denounce a dance in three countries at once, everybody wants to see it. Buenos Aires read its own press from abroad and decided that the thing it had been embarrassed by was, in fact, Argentine culture. By the early 1920s tango was being played in the city's good theatres, and Julio de Caro's sextet was writing arrangements that assumed musicians who could read.

So the honest version is unromantic: a poor, mixed, mostly immigrant music was exported, approved of by foreigners, and re-imported as heritage. In 2009 UNESCO listed tango as intangible cultural heritage, jointly for Argentina and Uruguay, which is the correct number of countries.

Why the origin story matters when you play it

Knowing this changes how the notes come out. If you hear tango as elegant salon music you will play it upright and even. If you hear the candombe underneath it you will lean into the beat and let the bass drag slightly ahead of where it is written, which is what an Argentine rhythm section does instinctively.

It also explains why the repertoire survives being rearranged. This music was assembled by ear from whatever instruments were in the courtyard, so it has never been precious about line-up. A bandoneon quartet, a guitar, a piano in a hotel lobby, or — in our case in Singapore — an accordion and a double bass all work, provided the rhythm is honest. The tunes were built to be portable. They have been proving it since 1890.

Frequently asked

Where was tango invented, Argentina or Uruguay?

Both, and the question is slightly wrong. Tango formed across the Rio de la Plata region, in the working-class districts of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, which functioned as one cultural area with constant boat traffic. Musicians moved between them freely. UNESCO recognised tango in 2009 as shared heritage of Argentina and Uruguay.

How old is tango music?

Roughly 140 years. Recognisable tango was being played in Buenos Aires and Montevideo by the 1880s, the first published tangos appeared in the 1890s, and the first recordings were made in the early 1900s. The form most people picture, with bandoneons and a full orchestra, dates from the 1920s onwards.

Did tango really start in brothels?

Partly, but the claim is exaggerated. Tango was played wherever poor people gathered to dance: tenement courtyards, cheap dance academies, street corners, cafes and, yes, some brothels. The brothel story stuck because it was scandalous and because respectable society used it to dismiss the music for thirty years.
