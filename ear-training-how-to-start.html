Ear training: how to start

Ear training is learning to name what you hear. Intervals, chord quality, rhythm and function, with a practice routine that takes ten minutes a day.

2026-07-06

Short answer

Ear training is the practice of connecting what you hear to what you know: naming intervals, chords, rhythms and harmonic function by sound alone. It is built by short daily work rather than long sessions, and it improves faster when you sing what you are trying to hear.

What you are actually training

Ear training is not about having a good ear in some innate sense. It is about building reliable links between a sound and a name. Almost everyone can already tell a happy chord from a sad one; the work is in making that recognition specific, fast and correct under pressure.

Four separate skills hide under the single phrase. Interval recognition is naming the distance between two notes. Chord quality recognition is naming a chord as major, minor, diminished, augmented or one of the seventh types. Rhythmic dictation is writing down what you heard in time. Functional hearing is recognising what a chord is doing in a key, which is the most musically valuable of the four and usually taught last.

They improve at different rates and they need different exercises, so it pays to know which one you are practising on any given day rather than vaguely listening to things.

Relative pitch, absolute pitch, and which one matters

Absolute pitch, often called perfect pitch, is the ability to name a note with no reference. It is rare, appears to depend on early exposure, and is not necessary for musicianship. Plenty of major performers do not have it.

Relative pitch is the ability to identify notes and chords in relation to a reference you have been given or have established for yourself. This is the skill that lets you transpose, improvise, play by ear, hear your own intonation and follow a score. It can be learned by anyone at any age, and it is what every serious ear-training course is really building.

One caution: people with strong relative pitch sometimes anchor everything to a single remembered note, which works well until the piece modulates. The goal is flexibility, not a fixed reference point.

Intervals: the practical method

Song anchors are the standard beginner's device and they work. A rising minor third is the opening of Greensleeves; a rising major third opens Kumbaya; a rising perfect fourth is the wedding march; a perfect fifth is the Star Wars theme; a rising major sixth is the NBC chime; an octave is Somewhere Over the Rainbow. Choose one song per interval, keep it for life, and do not switch.

But anchors are a crutch you should plan to discard. The faster route is to hear the interval as a pair of scale degrees. A major sixth is not a sound in isolation; it is the distance from one to six. Once you hear scale degrees directly, intervals name themselves as a by-product, and you have gained the far more useful ability to hear where a note sits in a key.

Practise descending intervals separately. They are noticeably harder than ascending ones and most people neglect them, which shows up immediately in dictation.

Sing first, then play

The single most effective instruction in ear training is to sing. Not well, not loudly, just accurately enough. Singing forces you to produce the pitch internally before you hear it externally, which is the mechanism you are trying to build. Listening passively trains recognition slowly; singing trains it fast.

A basic routine takes about ten minutes. Play a tonic note. Sing the scale up and down. Sing one, three, five and back. Then have something play a random note in the key and sing your way to it from the tonic, naming the degree. Then reverse: sing a degree you have chosen and check it on the instrument.

Add rhythm work separately. Clap a written rhythm against a metronome, then hear a rhythm and write it down. Most students find that rhythmic dictation is their weakest area and the quickest to improve, because the vocabulary of common patterns is small.

Using your instrument as the check

Accordionists have an unusual advantage: the instrument never goes out of tune within a session, so what you hear is a stable reference. You can play a tonic chord on the Stradella bass, hold it with light bellows pressure, and sing or pick out melodies above it. Practising melodic dictation over a sustained drone teaches functional hearing quickly, because every note is heard in relation to a present tonic rather than a remembered one.

That same stability can hide a weakness. Because equal-tempered reeds are fixed, an accordionist is never forced to adjust a pitch by ear, so intonation listening has to be trained deliberately rather than acquired through necessity.

The double bass is the opposite case. Intonation is entirely in the player's hands, and the open strings E, A, D and G give four fixed reference pitches you can check against constantly. Playing a stopped note against the open string a fifth or fourth below, and listening for the beats to disappear, is ear training and intonation practice in the same movement. Thumb position, where the hand leaves the familiar lower geography, is where this matters most and where students discover whether their ear is genuinely leading their hand.

Ten minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday, and that is not a motivational slogan but an observation from every teacher who has watched it. We build it into weekly lessons at A&G Academy for exactly that reason.

Frequently asked

How long does ear training take?

Basic interval and chord-quality recognition usually becomes reliable within a few months of ten minutes daily. Functional hearing, meaning you can follow a progression by ear in real time, takes considerably longer, often a year or two. Consistency matters far more than session length, and progress is noticeably faster with singing.

Do I need perfect pitch to be a good musician?

No. Perfect pitch means naming notes without a reference, and it is rare and largely fixed by early childhood. Relative pitch, hearing notes in relation to a key or a reference note, is the skill that supports playing by ear, transposing, improvising and intonation. It can be learned at any age.

What is the best first ear training exercise?

Sing the scale degrees over a held tonic. Play a tonic chord, sustain it, then sing your way from the tonic to a note somebody plays for you and name the degree you landed on. It builds functional hearing directly, rather than teaching isolated intervals you then have to relate to a key.
