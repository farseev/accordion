Major and minor scales explained

Major and minor scales differ by the pattern of tones and semitones inside them. Here is the pattern, the three minor forms, and why the third matters.

2026-05-12

Short answer

A major scale is a fixed pattern of steps: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. A minor scale lowers the third, sixth and seventh degrees of that pattern. The lowered third is what your ear hears as the difference between bright and dark.

A scale is a pattern, not a list of notes

Most people first meet the major scale as the sound of do re mi, or as the white keys from C up to C. Both are true and neither is the useful definition. The useful definition is a pattern of distances.

Two words first. A semitone is the smallest ordinary step in Western music, the distance from any piano key to the very next one, black or white. A tone is two semitones. On the double bass, a semitone in the lower positions is about the space between adjacent fingers; a tone is about the space from the first finger to the third.

The major pattern, counting upward from the starting note, is: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. Apply it from C and you get C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C, which happens to be all white keys. Apply the identical pattern from D and you get D, E, F sharp, G, A, B, C sharp, D. The sharps are not decoration. They are what is required to keep the pattern intact, and the pattern is what makes the sound recognisably major.

The minor scale and its lowered third

The natural minor scale takes the same seven letters and lowers the third, sixth and seventh degrees by a semitone each. C major is C, D, E, F, G, A, B. C natural minor is C, D, E flat, F, G, A flat, B flat.

Of those three changes, the third does nearly all the emotional work. The distance from C up to E is four semitones, called a major third. The distance from C up to E flat is three semitones, a minor third. That single semitone is the whole difference between a sound most listeners call bright and a sound most listeners call dark, sad or serious. You can test it in about four seconds on any keyboard: play C, E, G, then play C, E flat, G. Nothing else changed.

It is worth resisting the shorthand that minor equals sad. A great deal of dance music, most tango, and a large slice of Eastern European folk repertoire lives in minor and is not sad at all. Minor is a colour, not a mood.

Why minor has three forms

Natural minor has one practical weakness. Its seventh degree sits a whole tone below the tonic, the home note. In C minor that seventh is B flat, and B flat does not lean towards C with any urgency. Music that wants a decisive arrival needs a note a semitone below home, one that sounds unfinished until it resolves.

So harmonic minor raises the seventh back up: C, D, E flat, F, G, A flat, B natural, C. The B natural pulls hard to C, and the dominant chord built on G becomes a major chord, which is what gives minor-key cadences their bite. The cost is the gap between A flat and B natural, three semitones, an interval that sounds distinctly exotic and is awkward to sing.

Melodic minor smooths that gap by raising the sixth as well when the line ascends: C, D, E flat, F, G, A natural, B natural, C. Traditionally it reverts to natural minor coming down, since a descending line has no need to lean towards the tonic. In jazz practice the raised form is often used in both directions. Three forms, one scale, each solving a different problem.

Relative and parallel

Two relationships are worth naming, because they get confused constantly. Relative keys share a key signature. C major and A minor both have no sharps or flats; the relative minor always starts a minor third below the major. They contain identical notes and differ only in which note the music treats as home.

Parallel keys share a tonic instead. C major and C minor both start on C but have different signatures, three flats apart. Composers switch between parallel keys for immediate dramatic effect, because the home note stays put while the colour swings. Schubert built half a career on it.

Scales at the instrument

On a chromatic button accordion or bayan, scale fingerings repeat across keys in a way that piano players find slightly unfair. Because the button layout is symmetrical, the shape of a major scale starting on C is the same shape starting on F sharp, moved bodily across the board. Learn the shape once and you own twelve keys, which is why button players often practise scales in what a pianist would consider brutal keys without noticing.

On the piano accordion the fingerings vary by key, as on a piano, but the left hand rewards the work: the Stradella board gives you the fundamental of each scale degree in a predictable geometry, and the tonic, subdominant and dominant of any key sit within three buttons of each other. Practise the right-hand scale and the left-hand chord row together and the key becomes a physical place rather than a fact.

On the double bass, scales double as intonation training, because you have no frets and nothing to hide behind. Play slowly, stop on each note, and check it against an open string wherever one is available: in G major, sound the open G and the open D against your fingered notes and listen for the beating to disappear. That is not a beginner exercise. Orchestral players at A&G Academy do it before rehearsals, and so does everyone sensible.

Frequently asked

What is the actual difference between major and minor?

The pattern of tones and semitones. Minor lowers the third, sixth and seventh degrees of the major scale by a semitone. The third does most of the audible work: a major third is four semitones above the tonic, a minor third is three. Play C, E, G and then C, E flat, G and you will hear it immediately.

Which minor scale should I practise?

Learn natural minor first, because it matches the key signature and requires no extra thinking. Then add harmonic minor, since it is the form that generates the chords you will actually meet in minor-key pieces. Melodic minor comes third, and matters most for melodic writing and for jazz improvisation.

How long does it take to learn all the scales?

With ten focused minutes a day, most adult learners have the common major keys secure within a few months and all twelve within a year or so. Button accordionists usually move faster because one fingering shape transfers across keys. Speed is not the goal; even tone and reliable intonation are.
