Looking after a double bass in the tropics

Open seams, swollen wood, corroding strings and the air-conditioning shock behind most of it. How to keep a double bass stable in a humid tropical climate.

2026-08-02

Short answer

Constant humidity is easier on a bass than dry air, but sudden swings between air conditioning and outdoor heat are what open seams and crack tops. Aim for stability rather than a target number, let the instrument acclimatise slowly in its case, and have seams checked once a year.

What humidity does to a glued wooden box

A double bass is thin carved wood held together with hide glue, and wood moves with moisture. Spruce and maple swell across the grain when they absorb water and shrink when they dry, and because the top and back have their grain running in a different direction from the ribs, the plates and ribs fight each other every time conditions change. The joints take the strain.

Steady high humidity is not the main enemy. Instruments have lived in Southeast Asia for a long time. What causes damage is change, and specifically fast change. A bass that goes from a 24 degree air-conditioned rehearsal room into 32 degrees and heavy outdoor air, or back, is asking its glue joints to absorb a large dimensional shift over a few minutes.

Hide glue is designed to fail before the wood does. That is a feature: a seam that opens is a cheap repair, whereas a top that cracks is an expensive one. So an opened seam is the instrument protecting itself, and the correct response is to get it closed rather than to feel that something terrible has happened.

The air-conditioning problem

Most instrument damage in a tropical city happens at the boundary between conditioned and unconditioned air. Air conditioning does not just cool, it dehumidifies aggressively, and a room held at 22 degrees can sit well below 50 percent relative humidity while the corridor outside is above 80. Carrying an instrument across that boundary several times a day is the single most stressful thing you can do to it.

The practical fix is to slow the transition down. Leave the bass in its bag or cover for twenty to thirty minutes after arriving somewhere, and let the case act as a buffer while the temperature equalises. Do not unpack immediately and start playing, and do not leave a bass standing in the direct blast of an air-conditioning vent or in front of a fan.

The other frequent culprit is a car. A bass left in a parked vehicle in the sun reaches temperatures that soften glue and can distort the neck angle. If you must transport a bass by car, do not leave it there.

What to check, and how often

Run a finger around the edge where the top meets the ribs, and around the back edge too. An open seam often reveals itself by feel before it is visible. Tap lightly along the edges: a closed seam sounds solid, an open one gives a dull buzz or rattle. Check the bottom and upper bouts especially, since those take the most movement.

Listen for new buzzes. A buzz that appears suddenly usually means an open seam, a loose purfling, a lifting fingerboard, or a fitting that has worked free, and it is worth finding rather than playing through. Look at the bridge from the side: if it has started leaning towards the fingerboard, which happens when tuning pulls it forward over weeks, straighten it or have it straightened before it warps.

Check the soundpost is upright when you look through the f-hole, and check the fingerboard edge for any gap where the glue has released. An annual look from a luthier is sensible for any instrument you play regularly, and twice a year is not excessive here.

Strings, fittings and metal

Humid air with salt in it corrodes strings faster than a dry climate does, and sweat on the hands accelerates it. Wipe the strings and the fingerboard after every session with a dry cloth. Windings that go dull and rough go false in pitch, and a false string is one that will not play in tune no matter how good your ear is.

Tuning machines need a drop of light oil occasionally so they do not seize. Bow hair absorbs moisture and goes slack in humid conditions, so a bow that was fine yesterday may need different tension today; always slacken the hair fully after playing, which matters more here than in temperate places. Rehairing comes round more often in the tropics, and hair that has gone limp and will not grip is telling you it is due.

Rosin behaves differently in the heat. Softer rosins turn gummy and can leave a sticky deposit on the string and the top, which then holds dirt. A harder rosin generally suits a hot climate better.

Storage, travel and the practical minimum

Store a bass upright on a stand or lying on its side in a padded bag, away from windows, external walls and vents. Do not lean it against a wall where it can slide. If a room is dehumidified hard, a case or a heavy cover slows the exchange usefully. If a room is unconditioned, that is generally fine, provided it is not somewhere the sun lands.

For flights, a hard case is not optional and even then the instrument is at risk, mostly from pressure and temperature rather than from knocks. Slacken the strings a little, protect the bridge, and accept that most bassists who travel frequently keep a laminated instrument for the purpose. A plywood bass genuinely does tolerate this climate and this treatment better than a carved one, which is why so many working players here own one.

None of this is difficult. Slow the transitions, wipe the strings, slacken the bow, check the seams, and get a luthier to look once a year. Instruments in Singapore that get that treatment last indefinitely; the ones that get stored in an air-conditioned bedroom and carried out into the heat twice a day do not.

Frequently asked

What humidity level should I keep my double bass at?

Aim for stability rather than a specific figure. Somewhere between roughly 45 and 65 percent is comfortable, but an instrument held steadily at 70 will fare better than one that swings between 40 and 80 daily. The variation does the damage, not the absolute level.

My bass has an open seam. Is that serious?

It is normal and usually straightforward to fix. Hide glue is meant to release before the wood cracks, so an open seam is the instrument protecting itself. Get it reglued reasonably soon, because playing on an open seam lets it spread and lets dirt into the joint, but do not panic about it.

Do I need a dehumidifier for a double bass in the tropics?

Usually not for the instrument's sake. Constant high humidity is tolerable; the risk is mould in a closed case and corroded strings and fittings. Air circulation and wiping the instrument down matter more than a dehumidifier. What you should avoid is storing it where air conditioning dries it out unevenly.
